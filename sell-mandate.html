Chapter 01 — The Current System
Buy. Borrow. Die.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a documented, legal tax strategy used systematically by the ultra-wealthy. It has a name — "Buy, Borrow, Die" — and it means the wealthiest individuals pay effective income tax rates lower than their cleaners, permanently, indefinitely, with full legal blessing.

Step 01
Accumulate Assets
Acquire stocks, property, and private equity. Never sell. Unrealised gains are not taxable events — the wealth grows tax-free, year after year.
Tax paid: $0
Step 02
Borrow Against Assets
Need cash? Don't sell — borrow. Banks lend billions at near-zero rates against the asset portfolio. Loans are not income. Not taxable. Bezos, Musk, Zuckerberg's actual salaries: $1.
Tax paid: $0
Step 03
Live on Loans
Use borrowed money for yachts, jets, political donations, anything. Interest costs are often tax-deductible. The asset portfolio keeps growing, far outpacing interest rates.
Tax paid: $0
Step 04
Never Sell, Never Realise
Repeat for decades. Wealth compounds. Assets appreciate. Loans are repaid from dividends or new loans. The tax bill on $50 billion of gains: still zero, because nothing was "realised."
Tax paid: $0
Step 05
Die
Upon death, assets transfer to heirs at "stepped-up basis." The entire lifetime gain — $50 billion, $200 billion — is legally erased. Heirs start fresh. The cycle continues.
Tax on gain: $0 forever
ProPublica's 2021 investigation obtained IRS data showing that the 25 wealthiest Americans paid an average true tax rate of 3.4% over a five-year period, while their wealth grew by $401 billion. Warren Buffett paid 0.1%. Jeff Bezos paid 0.98%. None of this was illegal. It was the system working exactly as designed.

A wealth tax that allows borrowing against the same assets to pay the bill doesn't fix this. It is absorbed as a cost of doing business. The sell mandate closes the loop: the tax liability must be settled through asset sales. No exceptions. No loans against the taxed portfolio.

Not Cash. Never Cash.

When politicians talk about wealth taxes, they implicitly imagine the wealthy as holding large bank accounts. They don't. Ultra-high-net-worth portfolios are almost entirely non-cash. Here is the actual average composition, from Wealth-X and UBS portfolio data.

Cash & Equivalents
The only part a traditional "write a cheque" wealth tax can reach. On a $1 billion fortune: $20 million. A 2% annual tax on the whole fortune is $20 million — which equals 100% of their cash. Every year.
Actually liquid
The logical consequence: A 2% annual wealth tax without a sell mandate would require ultra-wealthy individuals to liquidate 100% of their cash holdings every single year just to pay it. In practice, they borrow to pay it, grow the debt against the appreciating assets, and the wealth concentration gets slightly worse, not better. Without compulsory divestment, the tax is theatre.

The verdict: The entire annual US sell mandate — $420 billion — is equal to slightly more than one day of normal US stock market trading volume. Distributed across 365 trading days, it represents 0.29% of daily volume. Academic research on large-scale share sales (privatisations, sovereign wealth fund rebalancing) shows that sell programs of this scale, distributed over months, produce price impacts of less than 0.5%. The "market crash" argument has no empirical basis.

Historical precedent: When Norway's sovereign wealth fund rebalances — regularly moving tens of billions — markets absorb it without crisis. When post-war Germany implemented wealth levies in 1952, stock markets recovered within months. The claim is a lobby argument, not an economic one.

Chapter 04 — The Economy Runs Faster
Money That Moves
Creates More

When assets transfer from indefinite holders to governments (who spend on services) or to working people (who spend nearly all income), the velocity of money increases. The same number of dollars circulates more times per year, generating more economic activity. This is not a political claim — it is undergraduate macroeconomics, and it appears in IMF modelling of redistribution effects.

— The core argument for the Sell Mandate
A person worth $10 billion who is required to sell $200 million in assets annually (2% per year) retains $9.8 billion after year one. After ten years of selling, they retain approximately $8.2 billion, assuming zero further appreciation. They remain among the wealthiest people in human history. Their children will never want for anything. Their heirs will never need to work. The sacrifice is purely numerical. The benefit to public budgets, housing markets, and democratic equality is structural and permanent.
The Objections — Answered
Every Counter-Argument

These are the standard objections raised in every wealth tax debate. Each has been examined and answered by economists. The conclusion is consistent: the objections are political, not economic.

📉
It will crash the stock market
"Forced selling of this scale would destroy market confidence and wipe out pension funds."
✓ The Answer
The annual US sell mandate ($420B) equals one day of US market trading volume. Distributed across 365 days, it is 0.29% of daily volume. Historical large-scale sales (Norway SWF rebalancing, post-war German wealth levies) show market impact below 0.5%. Pension funds, which own diversified index holdings, are not meaningfully exposed to individual large-cap price movements of this scale.
✈️
The wealthy will just leave
"They'll move to Monaco or Dubai and take their wealth with them."
✓ The Answer
Research on high-net-worth migration (Kleven et al. 2020) finds that fewer than 2% of UHNW individuals actually relocate when wealth taxes are introduced. Their businesses, social networks, families, staff, and infrastructure are here. A coordinated international approach (as the EU Tax Observatory proposes) eliminates the arbitrage entirely. The "flight risk" argument is the oldest lobbying claim in tax history — and the data doesn't support it.
🏗️
It will kill investment and innovation
"Entrepreneurs need large holdings to fund future ventures. Taxing success discourages it."
✓ The Answer
The sell mandate applies only to wealth above $100M. The first $100M is untouched. No entrepreneur needs more than $100M of personal wealth to fund a startup. The argument conflates corporate investment capital (which is not personal wealth) with personal fortunes. Norway, Denmark, and Sweden — all with significant wealth taxation — consistently rank among the most innovative economies on Earth.
🔢
Wealth is too hard to value
"Private companies and art can't be accurately valued annually. The administrative burden is impossible."
✓ The Answer
Switzerland has operated a net wealth tax for over a century on exactly this basis. The EU Tax Observatory's 2024 proposal for a global minimum wealth tax includes a mark-to-market valuation protocol for private assets using revenue-based multiples. "Too complex" is applied selectively — the same governments that claim this operate estate systems that value entire fortunes on death for inheritance tax purposes.
🏘️
Selling property would harm renters
"Forced property sales will disrupt the rental market and harm people who currently rent from large landlords."
✓ The Answer
Forced sale of property portfolios above a threshold transfers ownership from institutional investors and wealthy individuals to owner-occupiers — the very people currently renting. Rents are high because supply is constrained by concentrated ownership. Distributing ownership broadly increases housing supply available for purchase, driving prices toward wage-affordable levels. Short-term tenancy protections during transition are standard policy.
📰
It's never been done successfully
"France tried a wealth tax and abolished it. Sweden too. These things don't work in practice."
✓ The Answer
France's ISF failed because it lacked a sell mandate and international coordination — it was easily avoided through asset restructuring. Norway's net wealth tax remains in place and raises significant revenue. Switzerland's has operated for over 100 years. The EU Tax Observatory's global minimum wealth tax proposal is specifically designed to close the loopholes that doomed the French experiment. The lesson of past failures is "design it better," not "don't do it."
